### Genuine Strengths (brief)
The paper is unusually candid. It builds a positive control, shows that the measure fails to grade within some populations and says so, supplies a grid placebo and a gridless frontier for a non-smooth statistic, calibrates the intervals by a double bootstrap, reports a held-out per-phase schedule, and reports its own contrary results (0.38 on the tuning runs, 12% with a prompt stop, 50 against 383 decisions). The arithmetic I rechecked is consistent: for example 5.56% of 8,290 is 460.7 decision-equivalents; 419.1 of 460.7 is 91%; the phase rooms 0.43, 15.59 and 0.82 weight to 2.13 and 5.56/2.13 is 2.60; 0.0012 of 460.7 is 0.56 decisions. My challenges are about what these numbers mean, not about their arithmetic.

### Strongest Counter-Argument
The paper's only confirmatory result is a tautology, and everything that is not a tautology is exploratory, post hoc and unstable. A fixed-noise constant-velocity EKF on a straight road has a near-constant covariance. The paper says so in its introduction and proves it without ground truth (Fig. 2(b)): the tracker margin is 1.10 times the class average on 99.26% of rows, and a global scale is swept away by the equal-caution design. Any measure, however blunt or sharp, returns zero here, because the selected rows are the same. The oracle, the graded oracles, the double bootstrap and the 2.2-decision bound are machinery around a result already fixed by the filter's noise model. The "class average" itself is the mean of the tracker's own reported covariance, so the experiment asks whether a quantity that barely varies helps relative to its own mean.

What is not a tautology is the "room" and the braking prediction's "83%". The paper's own analyses dissolve both. Three scalars (one margin scale per phase) recover 89% of the room with no per-decision information; against that scheduled baseline 78 decision-equivalents remain and the braking prediction's gain is 50, not 383. The room sits in 1,433 pre-stop decisions (91%) plus about 132 post-stop decisions where a 2 Hz, 0.5 s-step tracker lags a stop, with no latency or update-rate sensitivity run. Its size depends on recording length (8.58 to 5.56 points), on caution accounting (15.59 to 5.64 while the lead brakes) and on the recording policy (1.18 points and 0.38 on the only other run set). A reader gets a confirmed non-result and a set of conditional, unstable magnitudes on one scenario in one simulator, never tested closed loop.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. The room is a cost of the one-scale sweep and of 2 Hz tracker lag, not of missing uncertainty information. A margin scale scheduled on a phase estimate, or an ordinary faster tracker, would remove most of it; the paper gets this far (89% to 99.9%) but keeps the unscheduled room as the yardstick.
2. A bias-versus-variance explanation. In the braking phase the tracker's error is systematic (mean −3.21 m at 1 s, RMS z 3.2), which a variance cannot encode unless inflated by a manoeuvre-aware filter. The oracle's hindsight squared error mixes bias and variance, so a "perfect covariance" ceiling is unclear. An oracle limited to the conditional variance given the tracker's state would be the fair ceiling for the question the title asks.
3. A comparison against a simple, non-Kalman covariance policy: a covariance estimated online from the tracker's own innovation history across the whole track, or a covariance scheduled on speed or range, instead of one NIS-inflation rule that can only inflate a covariance already too wide (NIS 0.24 against 2).
4. A reaction-latency or update-rate sweep (2, 5, 10, 20 Hz), which would show whether the room and the 83% survive an ordinary tracker.

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives
- Occupants and following traffic bearing the cost of the unconditional stops that the 12% reading relies on (unmeasured; Table S36 gives only reliance).
- Planner and perception stack integrators whose real tracker updates at 10 Hz or more and whose covariance is adaptive (the one scenario where the question is live).
- Reviewers of simulation-based safety evidence who would want severity-weighted outcomes rather than binary contact.

### Unexamined Premise
The paper assumes that a planner's decision quality can be scored open loop on states visited by a different, crash-prone policy (5 of 35 evaluation runs and 10 of 20 tuning runs ended in contact) and that the resulting "room" is a property of the margin source rather than of that policy's state distribution. The paper states the off-policy caveat repeatedly, but the sole test of it is a confounded two-run-set comparison (M5) that disagrees by a factor of 4.7 on the room. The central quantity's meaning rests on this unsupported premise. This is carried in M5, so no separate row is added.

### Observations (Non-Defects)
- The paper's candour (Section V-C, VI, Table S4) is a model for reporting exploratory work; my C1 concerns the weight the exploratory content can bear, not the integrity of reporting.
- The grid placebo is a good idea and answers the earlier "tracker is 0.0012" concern well: the tracker's share against the placebo is −0.0003 [−0.0009, 0.0001].
- The manuscript is 12,451 words in total; the body without tables, captions and references is 8,926 words, above the 10-page recommendation, with a 34,240-word supplement. A reader needs the supplement to evaluate nearly every claim, which an OJ-ITS reviewer may find hard to audit.
