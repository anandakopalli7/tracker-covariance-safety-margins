**Strongest counter-argument.** The decision-level null is settled before a single decision is scored, and the experiment contains none of the conditions the paper gives as its reason for scoring it. CANMOT's noise is fixed; 8,280 of 8,290 evaluated decisions, and all 755 taken while the lead brakes, sit on tracks older than 4 s; and in that phase the tracker's stated deviation takes five values, 0.400 to 0.545 m, consistent with one state-time covariance propagated over state ages of 0 to 0.4 s that the class average shares. Section V-A's own explanation then entails the outcome: a near-constant multiple of the class-average margin selects the same rows once the scale is swept. The decision scoring cannot return anything that the ratio histogram of Fig. 2(b), a property of the filter, has not already stated.

What the scoring does reveal tells against the evaluation design more than for it. The oracle is a near-perfect outcome indicator, so finding room is close to guaranteed. The graded oracles grade information pooled but score below the class average within the lead-braking phase and phase-weighted, and Section V-C attributes 89% of the pooled room to knowing, from logged truth, which phase a decision is in. The measure is thus shown to be sensitive to phase, the one kind of information a steady-state covariance cannot carry by construction, and not to per-decision error within a phase, the kind that would make a tracker covariance worth having.

On this reading the paper reduces to a known analytic property, confirmed, plus post hoc demonstrations that an open-loop score's size depends on the commitment and on how long recordings run after the lead stops, quantified with an in-sample per-phase envelope on one simulated test bed. These are useful cautions; they are not the decision-level test the title promises.

**Claim-by-claim audit (Abstract, Section I, Section VI, Section VII).** I traced every number to its table: the room, 5.56 points with calibrated interval [4.43, 6.68] (Table S16); the tracker covariance's share, 0.0012 with calibrated interval [-0.0016, 0.0017]; the 99.26% band (Table V); 89% (Table S23); 83% and 29% (Table S22); 12% (Table S19); 50 against 383 decisions (Table S23); the 2.6 ratio (5.56 over the decision-weighted phase mean 2.13, Table S21); the bound of fewer than 6 decisions on the tracker covariance's frontier distance (Tables S1, S24, S24b); and the per-family ratios (Table S26). I found no data-conclusion mismatch. Every weakness below concerns what the numbers are taken to mean, not the numbers.

**Is an expected null a contribution?** Only weakly, and the paper half concedes this. Section I says a null is expected and justifies measuring it because the covariance also depends on detections, misses and track age; the Scope then notes that track births are absent (10 of 8,290). The consequence the paper does not draw is that, with those conditions absent, the decision-level null is implied by the margin ratio and the sweep (MAJOR row 1). Supplement S9 independently shows that the tracker's deviation ranks the realised error no better than the class average's while the lead brakes (rho 0.453 against 0.458), which settles the null for this tracker without any frontier. What remains contributory is the apparatus (contribution 1) and the diagnostic (contribution 4); the tracker result is a worked confirmation.

**Does Section V-C undercut the paper's own framing?** Yes, in three ways. First, the room was introduced as the value of perfect per-decision information and as the positive control that separates a null from a blunt measure; Section V-C shows that about 89% of it is recovered by knowing the phase, so the control mostly certifies sensitivity to phase allocation (MAJOR row 2). Second, the 89% is an in-sample lower envelope over about 8.2 million scale triples with phase labels from logged truth, not a quantity obtained by knowing only the phase (MAJOR row 3). Third, by Section V-C's own logic the pooled value lies in allocating caution across sub-populations of different risk, while the evaluation sweeps one scale within one scenario family; that removes by construction the one channel through which this covariance differs from a class constant, its level across families (median ratio 1.103 in lead braking against 0.883 and 0.915 in the crossings, Table V). Section V-E says this cross-family effect was not tested and Section VI disclaims deployment advice, so I keep the third point in prose rather than as a row; it still makes the null a within-family statement.

**Cherry-picking among the added analyses.** The body does not suppress unfavourable added results; the selectivity is at the level of the abstract. The ranking of the two simple sources is mixed: the braking prediction leads under the 3 s commitment (pooled, with the ego moving, while the lead brakes) and pooled under the 0.75 s stop, whereas the acceleration margin leads before the lead's stop (0.43 against 0.29, Table S22), with the ego moving under the 0.75 s stop (0.26 against 0.04, Table IV), with signed caution there (0.07 against -0.90, Table S20c) and under a 2 m gap (0.80 against 0.58, the latter not distinguishable from zero, Table IV). The abstract reports only the braking prediction. The abstract also headlines post hoc analyses and drops a plan-fixed sentence (MAJOR row 5). The emphasis point alone is minor.

**Stale or contradictory wording between main text and supplement.**

- Supplement S8.2 still reads the pooled stop-scoring result as coming from caution spent across phases, "a mechanism that no analysis here isolates", although Section V-C and Table S23 now isolate it and Table S4 records that phrase as replaced.
- Supplement S8.2 also says "the location of the room under the 3 s commitment (Section VI) does not hold"; Section VI no longer states a location of the room.
- Table S4's rows on the NIS-inflated covariance in the abstract describe abstract wording that the current abstract does not contain (part of MAJOR row 5).
- Section V-B explains the NIS-inflated covariance's inaction "because it can only inflate and the covariance is already too wide", but Supplement S9 shows the planner's propagated deviation is too narrow while the lead brakes (RMS z 3.17 at 1 s, 4.77 at 2 s, 5.89 at 3 s). The statement holds for the innovation covariance at updates, not for the margin at planning look-aheads where the lead-braking room lies.

**Stakeholder premises.** Who benefits: integrators who could replace a per-track covariance margin with a class constant, and the evaluation community, which gains a decision-level protocol. Who bears the cost: road users behind the ego, who absorb the unconditional 6 m/s2 fallback stop that the reaction-and-stop scoring assumes and the extra braking of the braking prediction, and occupants, whose comfort is unscored. Who is left out: pedestrians, cyclists and crossing traffic, the families where the ratio check shows the covariance does vary. The paper states these exclusions, and Section VI warns against removing covariance margins from a deployed stack. One premise is load-bearing and unsupported: that under a reliable stop the margin source matters mainly for comfort and progress (MAJOR row 6).

**Minor issues (not decision-bearing on their own).**

- The abstract's "On 35 runs not used to design the margins" is accurate for the margins, but the room and the tracker covariance's difference from the class average had been examined on those runs before (Section III-A); the abstract implies a cleaner hold-out for the headline null than exists.
- The abstract closes by asking open-loop scores to state their commitment and population, but does not state its own population mix (82.7% of decisions after the lead's stop, 77.5% with the ego at or below 0.5 m/s), although the room ranges from 5.56 to 10.65 points with the after-stop segment kept (Table S22).
- Fig. 2(a) shows the oracle's frontier near zero from about 2.5% caution onward, so the room is in effect the mean height of the class average's frontier over caution levels up to about 27% of desired speed, and each share is close to a proportional reduction of that mean. The uniform weighting of operating points no planner would use is not defended; the shares depend on it, the null does not. This is for the methodology seat.
- Contribution 4's "nearly constant" is a row-pooled statistic. For the families to which the expectation is extended (stopped vehicle, cut-in), the paper does not show that the decision-critical rows lie inside the 5% band, which it did check for lead braking (Supplement S10.7).

**Ignored alternative explanations and paths.**

1. The analytic route: the steady-state Riccati covariance of CANMOT's fixed noise and its ratio to the class average give the null without simulation. The paper cites the mechanism but does not use it as a baseline against which the decision-level result adds anything.
2. A constant time-gap spacing policy, the default in adaptive cruise control, is absent from the comparators (acknowledged in Section VI); the per-phase result suggests a simple state-dependent spacing rule could capture much of the room.
3. A held-out or estimated-phase per-phase-scale rule: triples chosen on the 20 tuning runs, or phases estimated from tracked speed and acceleration, in place of truth labels and an in-sample envelope.
4. Populations where this covariance does vary (track births, missed updates, the re-initialised run, injected detection dropouts), the only places where a decision-level test of a fixed-noise covariance could return something the ratio does not.
5. The braking prediction's pooled 83% read as phase indication rather than braking anticipation: it changes margins only while the lead moves with a nonzero acceleration estimate, and its gain falls from 383 to 50 decisions against a per-phase class average. The revision has largely adopted this reading.

**Missing stakeholder perspectives.**

- Road users behind the ego (fallback stops and the braking prediction's extra deceleration).
- Ego occupants (jerk-limited emergency stops; the caution cost of each source).
- Vulnerable road users and crossing traffic (excluded; the covariance varies there).
- Safety assessors and integrators who would act on the ratio diagnostic.

**Unexamined premise.** That scoring decisions adds information about a fixed-noise filter in steady following beyond its steady-state covariance. The paper names the conditions under which it would (births, misses, track age) and then evaluates a population with none of them. The premise is load-bearing for contribution 3 and is MAJOR row 1.

**Observations (non-defects).**

- Supplement S9's rank-correlation check is the most direct evidence for the null and could be promoted to the main argument.
- The revision's conditional reading of the commitment (3 s against a 0.75 s stop) is better supported than the earlier location-of-the-room claim it replaced.
- The ratio diagnostic is cheap and useful as a screen, provided its row-pooled form is complemented by a check on decision-critical rows.

**Score basis for D3.** The abstract's numbers reproduce, the central null survives every scoring, and the conditional reading of the commitment is supported in direction by evidence independent of the per-phase envelope (pooled room 2.6 times the decision-weighted phase rooms). The gaps below narrow contributions 1 to 4 and correct how the positive control, the 89% and the reliable-stop reading are described. That is my Phase 1 warn condition: localised coherence gaps that leave the central argument standing. No single gap invalidates the core claim, so no row is CRITICAL.
